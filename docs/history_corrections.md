researcher was assigned to each: read the whole essay for context, then check every date,

work to Wikipedia.

Findings then went to a second pass instructed to **refute** them. This matters more than
the fan-out: the essay hedges deliberately in many places, and a fact-checker with a search
engine and no self-skepticism will confidently "correct" a careful hedge into a mistake.
Across the reviewed clusters that second pass **dismissed 104 of the 188 proposed corrections**, which is the system working rather than a waste.
Dismissed items are kept below, per cluster, because knowing what was checked and found
sound is as useful as knowing what was wrong. The second pass also checked the *suggested
revisions*, on the principle that a correct diagnosis with a wrong fix is its own failure.

Every cluster below carries the strongest grade the process produces: **adversarially
verified**, meaning an independent agent re-researched each correction from scratch,

### A — cosmos and prehistory ("From Stardust to Speech" / "The Long Road to Language")

Every factual claim in lines 28 to 38 was checked against primary or independent sources, first by the researcher and then by a skeptic working from separate fetches. #### Factual error · line 30

> sulfur, gold, uranium, and dozens of other elements that can be made only in the crush of a collapsing star or the violence of its death

The exclusivity is wrong twice over. Roughly half of all nuclei heavier than iron are built by slow neutron capture inside low- and intermediate-mass stars and shed on stellar winds, with no collapse and no explosion; those stars end quietly as white dwarfs. Gold, uranium and the rest of the rapid-neutron-capture elements are, on present evidence, made largely in neutron-star mergers, which are collisions of stellar corpses rather than the death of a star, with magnetorotational supernovae and magnetar giant flares also contributing. The site question is genuinely open, but no reading of the literature supports "only." Sulfur itself is fine, an alpha element from massive-star oxygen burning plus Type Ia.

**Suggested revision**

> sulfur, gold, uranium, and dozens of other elements that no star makes in its long middle age, forged instead in the crush of collapse, in the winds of dying giants, and, for the heaviest, in the collision of two neutron stars

**Evidence**

- Kobayashi, Karakas & Lugaro, ApJ 900, 179 (2020), arXiv:2008.04660: s-process in AGB stars reproduces Ba and Pb, and "Neutron star mergers can produce r-process elements up to Th and U, but the timescales are too long to explain observations at low metallicities" — i.e. two non-collapse channels plus an unsettled r-process site.
- Cowan, Sneden, Lawler et al., Rev. Mod. Phys. 93, 015002 (2021), arXiv:1901.01410: the r-process was historically "associated with supernovae" but "more recent studies have cast substantial doubts on this environment"; viable sites are neutron-star mergers, NS–BH mergers, and possibly rare supernova classes.
- GW170817 / AT2017gfo kilonova: a single binary-neutron-star merger may have produced between 3 and 13 Earth masses of gold (Metzger et al. review, arXiv:1901.09044).

**Verification.** High confidence; the skeptic upheld the error but rejected the researcher's proposed fix, which credited neutron-star mergers with "most" of the heavy elements. That would have replaced one overstatement with another, since the s-process share comes from winds and merger dominance is still contested. The revision above therefore names three sites and crowns none.

#### Needs hedging · line 30

> Nearby supernovae, the explosive deaths of massive stars, had seeded the cloud with heavy elements forged in stellar furnaces.

Two separate softenings are owed. Roughly half the carbon and nitrogen in the solar mix comes from low- and intermediate-mass stars on the asymptotic giant branch, which release their products on winds rather than in explosions, and roughly half the iron comes from Type Ia supernovae, which are exploding white dwarfs, not massive stars. Separately, "nearby" supernovae as the specific agent that enriched the presolar cloud is contested: the short-lived-radionuclide pattern in meteorites, especially aluminium-26 relative to iron-60, is hard to fit with a supernova, and Wolf-Rayet wind bubbles are the leading alternative. Oxygen, calcium, phosphorus and sulfur are legitimately massive-star products.

**Suggested revision**

> Generations of dying stars had seeded the cloud with heavy elements forged in stellar furnaces, some shed quietly on the winds of aging giants, the rest blasted outward by supernovae, the explosive deaths of stars.

**Evidence**

- Kobayashi, Karakas & Lugaro, ApJ 900, 179 (2020), arXiv:2008.04660: "Roughly half of the light elements such as C, N and F are produced by low- and intermediate-mass stars during their asymptotic giant branch (AGB) phase"; "half of the iron-peak elements ... are produced by Type Ia Supernovae, which are the explosions of C+O white dwarfs in binary systems."
- Karakas & Lattanzio, Dawes Review 2, PASA 31 (2014), arXiv:1405.0062: "Low and intermediate mass stars release their nucleosynthesis products through stellar outflows or winds, in contrast to massive stars that explode as core-collapse supernovae."
- Recent short-lived-radionuclide reviews (2022–2024) place the solar system's aluminium-26 in the parent molecular cloud, "most likely dominated by ejecta from Wolf-Rayet stars," and find direct supernova injection into the disk hard to achieve self-consistently.

**Verification.** Medium-high confidence, upheld by the skeptic at "roughly half" rather than the researcher's stronger "most of the carbon and nitrogen." The skeptic also warned that the researcher's replacement erased supernovae entirely, which would strand the "supernova-gifted" callback two paragraphs later, so the revision keeps the supernova image and simply stops making it the sole source.

#### Needs hedging · line 38

> By 45,000 years ago, humans had reached the Pontic-Caspian steppe

The 45,000 figure traces to the 2007 reading of the Don River sites at Kostenki, which a 2019 redating pulled back. The earliest Kostenki assemblage that is diagnostically modern human is about 41,000 to 40,000 cal BP, and the older dates there cannot be tied to diagnostic material. The oldest directly dated human remains in the region, Kostenki 14, are 38,700 to 36,200 cal BP, and Buran-Kaya III in Crimea is 38,000 to 36,000. Humans genuinely were around at 45,000 years ago at Bacho Kiro in Bulgaria and at Ust-Ishim in western Siberia, which bracket the steppe on both sides, so a reader is not badly misled, but 40,000 is the number the steppe evidence itself supports.

**Suggested revision**

> By 40,000 years ago, and quite possibly earlier, humans had reached the Pontic-Caspian steppe

**Evidence**

- Dinnis, Bessudnov, Reynolds et al., J. Hum. Evol. 127 (2019), PMID 30777356: Kostenki 17/II is ~41–40 ka cal BP, and "Dates of ≥41 ka cal BP from other Kostenki sites cannot be linked to diagnostic archaeological material, and therefore cannot be argued to date AMH occupation."
- Seguin-Orlando et al., Science 346 (2014): the Kostenki 14 individual, the oldest human remains in the region, directly dated 38,700–36,200 cal BP.
- Anikovich, Hoffecker et al., Science 315 (2007) 223, the source of the 45,000 figure, which hedged even then at "probably representing modern humans" with no human fossils anchoring it.

**Verification.** The skeptic verified the 2019 quotation directly and upheld the finding, but softened it from error to hedging: the redating demotes the old dates rather than disproving them, and 45,000-year-old humans at Bacho Kiro and Ust-Ishim make the sentence defensible as a round narrative number. Note for the record that the researcher misattributed the 2019 paper to "Bataille, Douka, Higham"; the authors are Dinnis, Bessudnov, Reynolds and colleagues.

#### Factual error · line 42

> Between roughly 4500 and 2500 BC, a people we call the ~Yamnaya~ inhabited the Pontic-Caspian steppe.

The Yamnaya archaeological horizon appears around 3300 BC and runs to roughly 2600 or 2500 BC. The essay's end date is right; the start date is off by about 1,200 years, and 4500 BC belongs to the Eneolithic predecessors of the same steppe, Khvalynsk and Sredny Stog, a millennium or more before the first Yamnaya kurgan. I went looking for a chronology generous enough to rescue the essay's number and there is none: Anthony's own review of the dispute lists the competing published start dates as 3800, 3300 and 3000 BC, and the earliest outlier position in the field, Morgunova's, reaches only about 3900 BC. The word roughly cannot absorb a gap that size. The error also makes the paragraph contradict itself, because the next sentence calls the wheel recently invented and the earliest wheeled-vehicle evidence anywhere is mid-fourth-millennium, with the oldest steppe wagon graves at 3100 to 3000 BC.

**Suggested revision**

> Between roughly 3300 and 2500 BC, a people we call the ~Yamnaya~ inhabited the Pontic-Caspian steppe.

**Evidence**

- Lazaridis, Patterson, Anthony, Reich et al. 2025, Nature 639:132-142, 'The genetic origin of the Indo-Europeans', refetched at PMC11922553 and quoted verbatim: 'The Yamnaya archaeological complex appeared around 3300 BCE across the steppes north of the Black and Caspian Seas, and by 3000 BCE reached its maximal extent from Hungary in the west to Kazakhstan in the east.'
- Anthony 2021, 'Early Yamnaya chronology and origins from archaeology': the early Yamnaya phase has been assigned start dates of 3800, 3300 or 3000 BC; 43 radiocarbon dates on early Yamnaya individuals from 27 kurgan cemeteries across the Pontic-Caspian steppes give calibrated averaged midpoints of 3203-3107 BC; Mikhailovka level 2 begins about 3500 BC but most early Yamnaya kurgans begin nearer 3300 BC, which Anthony calls the safer date for the general beginning of the Yamnaya era.
- David Anthony, The Horse, the Wheel, and Language (Princeton 2007), 'Wagon Dwellers of the Steppe': Yamnaya horizon c. 3300-2500 BC, most rapid spread 3400-3200 BC.
- Wikipedia 'Yamnaya culture' as a corroborating tertiary index: 3300-2600 BC, with Khvalynsk 4700-3800 BC and Sredny Stog c. 4500-3500 BC occupying the slot the essay gives to the Yamnaya.

**Verification.** Adversarially verified. I refetched the Lazaridis 2025 sentence from PMC and it is quoted correctly in the original finding, word for word. I then tried to break the correction rather than confirm it, by hunting for a published early chronology that would make 4500 BC defensible, and found instead that the full range of scholarly proposals runs 3900 to 3000 BC, which puts the essay outside it under every reading. The proposed revision is sound and preserves the ~Yamnaya~ markup. Nothing downstream breaks: line 76 dates the outward expansion to around 3000 BC, which sits inside the corrected range. Highest confidence in the cluster.

#### Needs hedging · line 42

> The Yamnaya were among the first to ride the domesticated horse, and that skill, combined with the recently invented wheel, gave them a mobility no earlier people had enjoyed.

Yamnaya horsemanship is a live and unusually sharp dispute, and the essay picks a side flatly. Trautmann and colleagues read a six-trait horsemanship syndrome off five Yamnaya skeletons and call them the oldest humans identified as riders so far, and Anthony, Trautmann and Heyd have since defended that reading at length. Against them, Hosek, James and Taylor argue in the same journal that the skeletal traits cannot be tied unequivocally to riding at all, and Librado and Orlando's horse genomics has the modern domestic lineage DOM2 dispersing only around 2200 to 2000 BC, with results that explicitly reject the association between riding and the Yamnaya expansion. Both camps are publishing in Science Advances and neither has won. The weakest word in the sentence is domesticated, since which horses were available to be ridden in 3000 BC is exactly what the genetics contests. The wagon half of the sentence is solid and carries the mobility claim on its own.

**Suggested revision**

> The Yamnaya may have been among the first to ride the horse, a reading some of their skeletons support and other specialists dispute, and whether or not they rode, the recently invented wheel gave them a mobility no earlier people had enjoyed.

**Evidence**

- Trautmann, Frinculeasa, Hakenbeck, Heyd et al. 2023, Science Advances 9:eade2451, 'First bioanthropological evidence for Yamnaya horsemanship', refetched at PMC10954216: five individuals well dated to 3021-2501 cal BCE from kurgans in Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary with at least four of six traits, 'the oldest humans identified as riders so far'. The authors' own caveat, in their words: 'Each single trait as described above may not be a specific occupational marker for horse riding.'
- Hosek, James and Taylor 2024, Science Advances 10:eado9774, 'Tracing horseback riding and transport in the human skeleton': the standing peer-reviewed critique of precisely this inference, on the ground that there are vanishingly few instances in which a particular activity can be tied unequivocally to skeletal change. This is the source the correction should have cited all along.
- Librado, Khan, Orlando et al. 2021, Nature 598:634-640: DOM2 arose in the lower Don-Volga and dispersed only c. 2200-2000 BC, and the results reject the commonly held association between horseback riding and the Yamnaya expansion into Europe around 3000 BCE.
- Against the correction, and the reason it is graded hedging rather than error: Anthony, Trautmann and Heyd 2026, Science Advances 12(20), 'Horse genetics, archaeology, and the beginning of riding', argue that horses of several lineages were managed, milked and ridden long before 2200 BCE and that Yamnaya groups of c. 3200-2600 BCE rode them.

**Verification.** Adversarially verified, and this is where the independent pass earned its keep. The finding survives but its evidence was materially wrong. The original entry cited 'Taylor et al. 2025, Science Advances, Horse genetics, archaeology, and the beginning of riding' for the proposition that riding postdates Yamnaya, flagged only as paywalled and read through summaries. That paper is not by Taylor and does not say that: it is Anthony, Trautmann and Heyd 2026, Science Advances 12(20), and it argues the opposite, that Yamnaya groups rode. A source cited against the essay was in fact a source for it. I have replaced it with the real critique, Hosek, James and Taylor 2024, which is what the researcher appears to have been reaching for. The correction still stands, because Hosek 2024 and Librado 2021 are enough to make the question contested and the essay states it flat, but the dispute is now demonstrably alive on both sides and the hedge must not tip the other way. I also rewrote the suggested revision, which was wrong twice: it blamed 'horse geneticists' for disputing a skeletal reading that bioarchaeologists dispute, and it hedged the riding while leaving 'that skill' asserting the mobility that follows from it. The replacement attributes the mobility to the wagon, which nobody contests.

#### Needs hedging · line 46

> Thirteen Yamnaya individuals have been genotyped directly for the European lactase-persistence allele, and not one carried a copy.

The direction of the claim is solidly sourced and the qualifier directly is well chosen, since the older apparent signal of the allele on the steppe came from imputation against a modern reference panel rather than from direct genotypes. The number is the problem. The best direct statement in the literature reports 48 individuals affiliated with Yamnaya-associated cultures screened at rs4988235 with no lactase-persistent individual among them. No source gives thirteen. The counts that actually circulate in this literature are five, the imputed Allentoft steppe signal the essay's word directly is written to exclude, and 48, the direct screen. The nearest thirteen is a count of 13 T-alleles out of 280 across all of Eurasia in the 4,000 to 3,000 BP window, a different quantity entirely.

**Suggested revision**

> Dozens of Yamnaya individuals have been genotyped directly for the European lactase-persistence allele, and not one carried a copy.

**Evidence**

- Segurel, Guarino-Vignon, Marchi, Heyer et al. 2020, PLOS Biology 18(6):e3000742, refetched at PMC7302802 and quoted verbatim: 'We don't see any LP individual among those directly affiliated with Yamnaya-associated cultures, but there are not many of them (48 individuals).'

- Wilkin et al. 2021, Nature 598:629-633, refetched at PMC8550948, is not the source of a thirteen either: it reports no lactase-persistence genotypes of its own, citing prior work only for the statement that the allele was rare in Early Bronze Age steppe populations.
- The competing figure in circulation is five: the steppe-origin hypothesis for lactase persistence rested on an rs4988235 frequency above 25 percent in five Yamnaya-associated individuals, imputed by Allentoft et al. 2015 rather than directly genotyped.

**Verification.** Adversarially verified and left at the sourcing audit's severity rather than promoted. #### Factual error · line 96

> The passive was ~synthetic~, formed not with a helping verb but with special endings, that same r inheritance shared with Celtic. ~Amatur~ meant ‘he is loved’ in a single word.

Classical Latin's passive was synthetic only in the infectum: present amatur, imperfect amabatur, future amabitur. The perfectum passive was periphrastic throughout, a perfect participle plus a finite form of esse, as in amatus est, amatus erat, amatus erit, and deponents ran on the same split, which is why they have three principal parts rather than four. The flat denial that a helping verb was involved is therefore false for half the paradigm, and it is false about the half that matters most to this essay, since the whole Latin-to-Romance narrative is built on the move from synthesis to analysis. Latin had already made that move in the passive perfectum, before Romance existed. What is not settled, and what the earlier pass's replacement prose asserted as though it were, is whether Spanish ser plus participle descends from that Latin periphrasis. The standard account says it does, by a reanalysis of the ambiguous amatus est. Danckaert argues on word-order evidence that the Latin perfect periphrases died out and the Romance present passives are a new formation. The correction should state the Latin fact and stop there.

**Suggested revision**

> The passive was ~synthetic~ in the unfinished tenses, formed not with a helping verb but with special endings, that same r inheritance shared with Celtic. ~Amatur~ meant ‘he is loved’ in a single word. The finished tenses needed a helper, and ~amatus est~, a participle beside the verb ‘to be’, is how Latin said ‘he was loved’.

The claim about -ís is exactly right, and is in fact stronger than the essay says, since ís is the third member of all four surviving sets.

**Suggested revision**

> In the Americas, far from the court, it survived, and most of it kept the monophthongs that had stood beside the plural's own endings since the fifteenth century: -ás beside -áis, -és beside -és, and -ís unchanged. Maracaibo kept the diphthongs and still says ~vos cantáis~.

#### Nitpick · line 202

The essay's next clause, about the devaluation of that respectful vos in Spain, makes the late dating implicit, and nothing about Spanish is at stake, so this is a one-word improvement rather than a correction.

**Evidence**

#### Nitpick · line 213

**Verification.** High confidence on the fact, low stakes on the correction, which is why it is a nitpick and a one-word fix.
